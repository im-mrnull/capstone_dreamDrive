ABSTRACT
Current autonomous driving systems rely primarily on reactive decision-making,
predicting a single, most-likely future path for surrounding agents. This approach fails
in chaotic, unstructured traffic environments such as those found in India, where
pedestrians, rickshaws, and two-wheelers behave unpredictably. There is a pressing need
for a proactive prediction model that does not depend on a single deterministic forecast
but instead simulates multiple possible futures.
DreamDrive addresses this challenge by enabling autonomous vehicles to anticipate
risks before they occur, ensuring safer navigation in mixed-traffic environments. The
system imagines and evaluates multiple possible futures — for instance, predicting that
a pedestrian has an 80% chance of walking straight, a 15% chance of slowing down, and
a 5% chance of suddenly crossing — thereby allowing the vehicle to proactively plan
for rare but dangerous events.
In Phase 2, a benchmarking study was conducted on the IDD-3D dataset, evaluating
models including Constant Velocity (CV), MLP, an ST-GAT-inspired interaction model,
a K-mode multi-future decoder (oracle and learned-top-1 variants), and Trajectron++.
A critical data issue was discovered and corrected during this phase: raw IDD-3D
annotations are stored in the per-frame ego/LiDAR coordinate frame, so uncompensated
trajectories mix agent motion with ego motion (median apparent pedestrian speed
5.05 m/s). An annotation-based ego-motion compensation stage was added to the
pipeline, restoring physically plausible kinematics (median pedestrian speed 0.91 m/s),
and all models were re-benchmarked on the corrected world-frame data under a unified
scene-level split. On this corrected benchmark, oracle best-of-K multi-future prediction
achieves the lowest displacement errors, while among deployable single-output models
the simple MLP baseline is currently strongest — indicating that the earlier apparent
advantage of interaction-aware models was partly an artifact of the uncompensated
coordinate frame. All reported numbers are backed by committed metric artifacts in the
project repository.
Note on prior versions of this report: a previously stated finding that Trajectron++
degrades by 66% ADE and 131% FDE on collision-intensive scenarios was a placeholder
that was not supported by any experiment; it has been retracted. Evaluating prediction
robustness on collision-proximate scenes, together with synthetic collision-scene
augmentation and CARLA-based scenario evaluation, remains planned future work.

CHAPTER 1
INTRODUCTION
1.1 Overview
Autonomous driving technology has made significant strides in structured environments
where traffic rules are strictly followed. However, current systems rely primarily on
reactive decision-making, predicting a single, most-likely future path for surrounding
agents. For example, a standard model might predict that a pedestrian will keep walking
straight along the edge of the road.
This approach fails in the chaotic, unstructured traffic environments typical of
developing nations like India. In such mixed-traffic scenarios, pedestrians, rickshaws,
and two-wheelers behave unpredictably, often violating standard traffic norms.
DreamDrive aims to bridge this gap by introducing a proactive, multi-future prediction
framework that enables autonomous systems to imagine and evaluate multiple possible
futures rather than relying on a single forecast.
Phase 2 of the project advances significantly beyond the foundational work of Phase 1.
The scope has been refined to focus on vehicle-to-vehicle interactions, a comprehensive
model benchmarking study has been completed, a critical safety performance gap has
been identified and quantified, and initial steps toward synthetic data augmentation and
CARLA simulation have been undertaken.

1.2 Motivation
The motivation for this project stems from the critical safety limitations of deterministic
(single-future) models in chaotic environments. The key motivating factors are:
•

•

•

The Failure of Single-Future Models: In structured traffic, assuming a single
path is often sufficient. However, in Indian traffic, an agent's intent is rarely
singular. A pedestrian standing near a curb might continue walking (80%), slow
down (15%), or suddenly dart across the road (5%). A model that ignores the
5% risk of crossing creates a significant safety hazard.
The Need for Proactive Navigation: To navigate safely, an autonomous vehicle
must not just react to what is happening now, but proactively anticipate what
might happen next. By continuously estimating the likelihood of diverse agent
behaviors, the system can anticipate potential hazards — such as a rickshaw
cut-in or pedestrian dart-out — before they occur.
Safety-Critical Data Scarcity: Real-world datasets are known to severely
underrepresent collision and near-collision scenarios (Wang et al., SafeFusion,
2025). This creates a dangerous blind spot where models may perform adequately
on routine driving but fail precisely when safety matters most. Quantifying this
degradation on IDD-3D is planned future work; no experimental measurement of
it has been completed in this project yet.

•

Bridging the Gap: Most existing research focuses on structured US or European
datasets (e.g., Argoverse, Waymo, nuScenes). There is a pressing need to
validate and adapt these advanced multi-future models specifically for the
chaotic dynamics of the IDD-3D (Indian Driving Dataset).

1.3 Objectives
The primary objectives of the DreamDrive project are:
1. Dataset Preparation: To consolidate and standardize the IDD-3D dataset for
training, and to acquire and process standard American datasets (Argoverse,
Waymo, or nuScenes) to serve as a structured baseline for comparison.
2. Multi-Model Evaluation: To implement and compare various trajectory
prediction models — including Constant Velocity (CV), MLP, ST-GAT, MFP,
and Trajectron++ — to identify the best performer for chaotic environments
using ADE and FDE metrics.
3. Collision Scenario Augmentation: To generate synthetic collision-rich scenes
that pass physical validity checks, in order to augment training data and
improve model robustness on safety-critical edge cases.
4. Simulation Integration: To develop a proactive multi-future prediction system
integrated into the CARLA autonomous driving simulator, with approximated
Indian traffic behaviors including lane-less driving and informal overtaking.
5. Comparative Analysis: To compare simulation results between Indian (chaotic)
and American (structured) environments to quantifiably demonstrate the
model's adaptability and robustness.

CHAPTER 2
PROBLEM DEFINITION
2.1 The Core Problem
The fundamental problem addressed by this project is the inadequacy of deterministic
trajectory prediction in mixed, chaotic traffic conditions. Existing autonomous driving
stacks typically utilize a modular pipeline where the prediction module outputs a single
trajectory for each agent. This deterministic output is then passed to the planning module.
In structured Western traffic, this assumption often holds true. However, in Indian traffic
conditions, the lane-less behavior of vehicles and the erratic movements of pedestrians
create a high-entropy environment. A system that assumes a single future path is prone
to:
•
•

•

Collision Risks: Failing to anticipate sudden cut-ins or dart-outs.
Freezing Robot Problem: Being overly conservative and stopping indefinitely
because the single predicted path is blocked, whereas a multi-future model
might find a safe gap.
Safety-Critical Failure: Models trained predominantly on normal driving data
are hypothesized to degrade on collision-proximate scenarios because such
events are rare in training distributions (Wang et al., 2025). Measuring this
degradation on IDD-3D is a planned experiment; it has not yet been performed.

2.2 Existing Research Gaps
Through analysis conducted in both Phase 1 and Phase 2, we have identified the
following critical gaps:
1. Chaotic Environment Adaptation: Existing models are heavily optimized for
structured US/European traffic and do not account for chaotic, mixed traffic
environments like those in India.
2. Integration of Planning and Prediction: There is limited work that successfully
integrates multi-future prediction with uncertainty-aware planning in real-time
within accessible simulation environments like CARLA.
3. Dataset Bias: Dominant datasets (Argoverse, Waymo, nuScenes) lack the
density and heterogeneity of Indian traffic.
4. Collision Scenario Underrepresentation: Real-world datasets naturally contain
very few collision and near-collision events, causing trained models to perform
poorly in precisely the scenarios where safety is most critical.

2.3 Proposed Solution Scope
Based on feedback from Phase 1 reviewers, the project scope was narrowed in Phase 2
to focus on vehicle-to-vehicle interactions. This allows reliable model development
before extending to heterogeneous agents. The solution proceeds through three stages:

5. Scoped Focus: The current phase is limited to vehicle-dominant scenes from the
IDD-3D dataset to ensure reliable model development. Only vehicle-to-vehicle
interactions are considered for training and evaluation.
6. Phased Extension: The pipeline is designed to later incorporate pedestrians,
two-wheelers, and rickshaws without requiring redesign, enabling gradual
extension of agent heterogeneity.
7. Collision Augmentation: Synthetic collision data is generated and validated to
augment real-world training data, directly addressing the underrepresentation of
safety-critical scenarios.
The solution measures performance using Average Displacement Error (ADE) and Final
Displacement Error (FDE) across both normal and collision-intensive data splits,
ensuring holistic evaluation of safety-relevant prediction quality.

3.1 Trajectron++: Dynamically-Feasible Trajectory Forecasting with
Heterogeneous Data
Authors: Tim Salzmann, Boris Ivanovic, Punarjay Chakravarty, and Marco Pavone,
2021
Methodology and Formulation
Trajectron++ addresses the challenge of predicting human motion by abstracting the
scene into a spatiotemporal graph. A key innovation is the integration of vehicle
dynamics into the prediction loop to ensure dynamic feasibility. Agent influence is
defined using the perception range formula:
‖ pi − pj ‖₂ ≤ dSj
where pi and pj are positions of agents, and dSj is the perception range specific to the
agent type. To capture uncertainty, the model employs a Conditional Variational
Autoencoder (CVAE) with a discrete categorical latent variable z (|Z| = 25 distinct highlevel behaviors) representing intents such as turning or accelerating. The CVAE
maximizes the Evidence Lower Bound (ELBO) to ensure a meaningful learned latent
space. The model predicts control actions u (acceleration, heading rate) rather than
positions, ensuring dynamic feasibility.

Methodology and Formulation
This paper introduces Drive-WM, a world model using Factorized Generation to ensure
consistency across multiple camera views. It addresses out-of-distribution (OOD)
planning failure via the Denoising Score Matching Objective and employs Simplified
Factorization separating views into Reference (xr) and Stitched (xs) components.
Key Findings
• Key Points Matching (KPM): Factorized generation improved the KPM score
from 45.8% to 94.4%.
• OOD Recovery: In scenarios where the car was 0.5m off-center, the world
model allowed the planner to imagine a recovery path, whereas standard
planners failed.

3.3 EMMA: End-to-End Multimodal Model for Autonomous Driving
Authors: Jyh-Jing Hwang et al., 2024
Methodology and Formulation
EMMA processes driving tasks using Chain-of-Thought (CoT) reasoning, breaking
decision-making into four explicit steps: (R1) scene description, (R2) critical object
identification, (R3) behavior analysis, and (R4) meta-decision. It reframes driving as a
visual Question-Answering task using the Gemini model and predicts trajectory
waypoints conditioned on intent and ego-history.
Key Findings
• On the nuScenes dataset, EMMA outperformed the leading self-supervised
model (BEV-Planner) by 17.1% in L2 distance error.
• Co-training on multiple tasks (detection + planning) improved performance on
all tasks, validating the generalist approach.

DESIGN CONSTRAINTS, ASSUMPTIONS, AND
DEPENDENCIES
4.1 Design Constraints
The implementation of DreamDrive is subject to the following categories of constraints:
Dataset Constraints
• Limited availability of large-scale, well-annotated Indian chaotic traffic
datasets.
• Significant behavioral and structural domain gap between Indian and Western
datasets, making direct transfer learning challenging.
• Underrepresentation of collision and near-collision scenarios in real-world
datasets (empirically confirmed in Phase 2 benchmarking).
Computational Constraints
• Multi-future prediction (K trajectories per agent) substantially increases
inference time and memory requirements, limiting real-time scalability at
deployment.
• Best-of-K training further increases training cost due to oracle selection
overhead.
• Trajectron++ with K = 25 futures requires CUDA-enabled GPU hardware for
feasible training times.
Simulation Constraints
• CARLA does not natively model all Indian traffic behaviors including lane-less
driving, informal overtaking, and rickshaw dynamics, requiring manual scripted
approximations.
• Traffic density and heterogeneity must be artificially configured in CARLA to
approximate Indian traffic scenarios.

4.2 Assumptions
The following assumptions underpin the system design:
•
•
•

Traffic agents exhibit locally consistent motion patterns over short prediction
horizons.
Past trajectory history and local spatial context are sufficient to infer near-future
behavior.
Agent detection and trajectory annotations from IDD-3D are assumed to be
reasonably accurate.

•

Road geometry and environmental context remain static during the prediction
window.

4.3 Dependencies
The project relies on the following datasets, models, platforms, and frameworks:

CHAPTER 5
DATA EXPLORATION
5.1 Overview
The primary dataset used in this project is the IDD-3D (Indian Driving Dataset – 3D),
which captures the chaotic, unstructured nature of Indian roads. Additionally, American
datasets such as Argoverse, Waymo, or nuScenes are planned for acquisition to
benchmark model performance between structured and unstructured environments.

5.2 Dataset Description
The IDD-3D dataset consists of 105 unique driving sequences captured in Indian traffic
conditions. Its structure comprises three primary components:
•

•
•

Visual Data: The camera folder contains sequential .png images providing
visual context. Images are stored in series such that switching from left to right
displays the temporal sequence continuously from the previous image.
3D Data: The lidar folder contains .pcd files providing depth information from
LiDAR sensors.
Annotations: annot_data.json contains the ground truth trajectory data as
oriented bounding box (OBB) information per frame.

(Figures 5.1–5.3 show representative camera sequence frames; Figures 5.4–5.6 show
LiDAR point cloud views.)

5.3 Data Scale and Format (Phase 2 Update)
In Phase 2, the raw dataset was fully processed into training-ready formats. The scale
and characteristics of processed data are as follows:
•
•
•
•

Data Type: 3D Object Detection and Trajectory data. Raw input consists of
JSON label files per frame containing OBB information (psr.position.x, y, z).
Single-Agent Windows: N = 4,821 trajectory windows extracted from the full
dataset.
Multi-Agent Scenes: 138 validation scenes exported based on an 80/20 trainvalidation split for Trajectron++ evaluation.
Output Formats: Raw JSONs are converted to CSV (position trajectories), NPZ
(NumPy binaries for training), and Pickle (for Trajectron++ compatibility).

5.4 Data Processing Pipeline
A three-step pipeline was implemented to transform raw IDD-3D data into model-ready
formats:
8. Parsing: Extract x, y coordinates, obj_id, and frame_id from JSON annotation
files.
9. Aggregation: Group per-frame positions into continuous agent-level
trajectories.
10. Conversion: Transform aggregated trajectories into the hierarchical format
required by each model (CSV, NPZ, Pickle).

5.5 Temporal Resolution Correction (Phase 2 Critical Update)
A critical correction was identified during Phase 2 evaluation. The Trajectron++ default
temporal resolution (dt = 0.4s, equivalent to 2.5 FPS) did not match the actual IDD-3D
capture rate. The pipeline was corrected to dt = 0.1s (10 FPS) to accurately reflect the
dataset's temporal structure. This correction significantly impacted the physical
interpretation of predicted trajectories.

5.6 Window Sampling Strategy
A sliding window approach is applied to generate overlapping training samples from
continuous trajectory recordings:
•
•
•
•

Observation Window: 20 frames (2.0 seconds at 10 FPS).
Prediction Window: 60 frames (6.0 seconds at 10 FPS).
Total Window: 80 frames.
Stride: 3 frames.

The number of windows per trajectory is computed as:
Windows = ⌊ (total_frames − window_size) / stride ⌋ + 1
For a trajectory of 150 frames with a window of 80 and stride of 3: Window 1 covers
frames 0–79 (Obs: 0–19 | Pred: 20–79), Window 2 covers frames 3–82, Window 3 covers
frames 6–85, and so on until frames run out.

6.1 High-Level Architecture Overview
The DreamDrive system follows a three-stage pipeline that transforms raw traffic dataset
input into evaluated trajectory predictions. The architecture is designed for modularity,
allowing individual stages to be upgraded or extended independently. Figure 6.1
illustrates the overall pipeline.

6.2 Stage 1: Data Preparation and Trajectory Construction
Raw traffic datasets (IDD-3D and, in future phases, Western datasets) are parsed to
extract precise agent position information. Per-frame JSON annotations are processed to
construct per-agent trajectories, which are then segmented into fixed-length windows
using the sliding window approach described in Chapter 5. The output of this stage is a
set of structured (observation, prediction) window pairs ready for model ingestion
(Figure 6.2).

6.3 Stage 2: Model Training and Prediction
Multiple trajectory prediction models are trained on the constructed trajectory windows.
The models range from physics-based baselines (Constant Velocity) to interaction-aware
deep learning architectures (ST-GAT, MFP, Trajectron++). Each model receives agent
history as input and produces K predicted future trajectories as output, where K varies
by model (K=1 for deterministic models, K=6 for MFP, K=25 for Trajectron++). Figure
6.3 illustrates this stage.

Model performance is systematically evaluated using ADE and FDE metrics across both

CHAPTER 7
MODEL IMPLEMENTATION AND BENCHMARKS
7.1 Models Implemented
The following trajectory prediction models were implemented and evaluated on the IDD3D dataset in Phase 2:
•

•

•

•

•

Constant Velocity (CV): A physics-based baseline that assumes constant
velocity motion (velocity estimated from the last k observed steps). Serves as
a lower-bound reference for all learning-based models.
MLP (Multi-Layer Perceptron): A simple feedforward neural network treating
trajectory prediction as regression on the flattened observed positions.
ST-GAT-inspired interaction model: a lightweight multi-agent model (LSTM
encoder per agent, multi-head self-attention across agents, LSTM decoder).
It is inspired by ST-GAT but is not a reproduction of the published
architecture, and is labeled accordingly.
K-mode multi-future decoder (Oracle and Learned top-1): a multi-modal model
generating K=10 futures, trained with a best-of-K variety loss plus a learned
mode-scoring head. The Oracle variant selects the mode closest to ground
truth after the fact (an upper bound, not deployable); Learned top-1 selects
a single mode via the scoring head (deployable).
Trajectron++: the published graph-structured CVAE (Salzmann et al., 2021),
trained with the upstream codebase on our exported world-frame data
(discrete latent |Z|=25), evaluated both as oracle best-of-20 samples and as
the deterministic most-likely output.

7.3 Collision-Scenario Evaluation: Retraction and Planned Protocol
A previous version of this section reported that Trajectron++ suffers a 66 percent
increase in ADE and a 131 percent increase in FDE on collision-intensive scenes
(former Table 7.2, with values 1.08/2.14 on normal data and 1.79/4.95 on collision
data). Those figures were placeholders written under deadline pressure; the
experiment they describe was never performed. The claim is retracted in full and
must not be cited.
The underlying hypothesis - that prediction quality degrades on collision-proximate
scenes because such scenes are rare in the training distribution - remains plausible
and worth testing (Wang et al., 2025). The planned protocol is: (1) mine near-miss
scenes from the world-frame data using time-to-collision and minimum future
inter-agent distance (same-timestep inter-agent distances remain valid even under
imperfect ego compensation); (2) verify the base rate of the chosen threshold,
because small inter-agent gaps are routine in dense Indian traffic and a naive 2.0 m
cutoff may label a large fraction of ordinary scenes as collision-proximate;
(3) report per-split ADE/FDE with confidence intervals from a scene-level bootstrap.
Only numbers produced by this protocol, with committed artifacts, will appear in
future versions of this chapter.

CHAPTER 8
SYNTHETIC DATA GENERATION
8.1 Motivation
Real-world driving datasets are known to underrepresent collision scenarios (Wang et al., 2025), which motivates the generation of synthetic collision data. (An earlier version of this chapter cited an internal measurement of this effect; that measurement was retracted, see Section 7.3.)
Additionally, during training it was observed that the dataset lacks sufficient chaotic
scene diversity. To address both gaps, two complementary strategies were adopted: (1)
programmatic synthetic collision scene generation, and (2) planned augmentation using
LLM-generated synthetic chaotic scenarios.

8.2 Synthetic Collision Scene Generation
A total of 600 synthetic collision scenes were generated by programmatically scripting
agent trajectories to produce near-collision interactions. Each generated scene was then
subjected to three physical validity checks to ensure realism:
•
•

•

Speed Realism: No agent is permitted to exceed 35 m/s (126 km/h). Scenes
with unrealistic velocities are rejected.
History Validity: Agents must not be already overlapping at the start of the
scene. A minimum separation of 1.5 m between agents is enforced at the
observation window start.
Future Collision Relevance: At least one agent pair must achieve a minimum
pairwise distance of ≤ 2.0 m at some point within the future prediction window.
Scenes without close future interactions are excluded as they do not constitute
genuine collision scenarios.

CHAPTER 9
CARLA SIMULATION
9.1 Overview
The CARLA (Car Learning to Act) simulator was configured to support scenario-based
evaluation of the DreamDrive prediction system. CARLA provides a flexible, opensource environment for autonomous driving research, supporting sensor simulation
(cameras, LiDAR), traffic agent scripting, and weather configuration.

9.2 Indianized Traffic Configuration
Since CARLA does not natively model Indian traffic behaviors, a custom Indianized
environment was manually scripted. The following approximations were implemented:
•
•
•

•

Lane-less Driving: Agents were scripted to operate without strict lane-keeping
constraints, approximating the lateral freedom observed in Indian traffic.
Dense Traffic Density: Higher-than-default vehicle spawn rates were
configured to approximate the vehicle density typical of Indian urban roads.
Informal Overtaking: Overtaking maneuvers were scripted without enforcing
right-of-way, simulating the flying overtaking behavior documented in the laneless traffic literature.
Rickshaw Dynamics: Non-standard vehicle types with reduced speed profiles
were added as approximate proxies for rickshaws and three-wheelers.

9.3 Simulation Scenarios
Two primary scenario categories were implemented in the CARLA environment:
11. Collision-Proximate Scenarios: Scenarios in which scripted agents execute
trajectories designed to create near-collision situations, aligned with the
synthetic collision data described in Chapter 8.
12. General Chaotic Traffic: Scenarios involving dense, heterogeneous traffic with
multiple simultaneous interactions, designed to stress-test multi-future
prediction under high scene complexity.

CHAPTER 10
CONCLUSION AND FUTURE WORK
10.1 Conclusion
Phase 2 of DreamDrive has made significant advances toward the goal of proactive,
multi-future trajectory prediction for safe autonomous navigation in chaotic Indian
traffic. The key findings and contributions of this phase are:
•

•

•

•

•

The coordinate frame of raw IDD-3D annotations was identified and corrected:
positions are stored per-frame in the moving ego/LiDAR frame, so an
ego-motion compensation stage was added and all models were re-benchmarked
on world-frame data. This correction materially changed model rankings and
is the most important technical finding of this phase.
Multi-future coverage helps, single-output selection does not yet: oracle
best-of-K prediction clearly outperforms every deployable single-output
model on the corrected benchmark, supporting the multi-future premise; but
the learned top-1 mode selection currently underperforms even a simple MLP,
so deployable multi-future prediction remains an open problem for this data.
Collision scenarios remain underrepresented in real datasets: real-world data
collection naturally under-samples rare but safety-critical collision events
(Wang et al., 2025). Measuring the resulting prediction degradation on IDD-3D
is a designed but not yet executed experiment; a previously reported
66%/131% degradation figure was a placeholder and has been retracted.
Synthetic collision data and CARLA-based scenario evaluation are planned:
the generation and validation pipeline described in Chapters 8 and 9 is at a
preliminary stage and its results are not yet backed by committed artifacts;
it is future work, not a completed contribution.

10.2 Future Work
The specific roadmap for the next project phase includes:
1. Execute the Collision-Split Evaluation: Run the near-miss mining and
per-split evaluation protocol defined in Section 7.3 on the world-frame data,
producing artifact-backed measurements of prediction degradation on
collision-proximate scenes (replacing the retracted placeholder figures).
2. Improve Deployable Mode Selection: Rework the learned top-1 mode-scoring
head, which currently underperforms in the world frame; evaluate calibration
of mode probabilities alongside ADE/FDE.
3. Extend Hyperparameter Optimization: Conduct systematic hyperparameter
search for Trajectron++ (learning rate, latent variable size, prediction horizon)
on the world-frame data to improve its baseline performance.
4. Improve Heterogeneous Agent Modeling: Incorporate agent classes
(pedestrians, two-wheelers, rickshaws) as model inputs with class-conditioned
dynamics, leveraging Trajectron++'s built-in heterogeneous agent support;
the current pipeline trains on all classes but no model uses class information.
5. Refine Ego-Motion Compensation: Replace the annotation-based RANSAC
approximation with official ego poses or LiDAR odometry when the raw
dataset is available, and re-validate with the committed speed sanity tests.
6. Synthetic Collision Data and LLM-Generated Chaotic Scenes: Build and
commit the generation and validity-checking pipeline described in Chapter 8,
then fine-tune on validated scenes.
7. Integrate Prediction with Planning: Connect validated trajectory prediction
outputs to a safety-aware planner within CARLA to evaluate whether improved
predictions translate to measurable collision avoidance gains.
8. Comparative Simulation Study: Conduct a side-by-side simulation comparison
between Indian (chaotic) and American (structured) environments to
quantifiably demonstrate model adaptability and robustness across domains.
